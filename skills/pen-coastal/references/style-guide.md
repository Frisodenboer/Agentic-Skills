# Pen-Coastal — Style Guide

> Derived from three close-up reference photos of a screen-printed coastal/nautical textile (`reference-Pen_Style_01.jpg` through `_03.jpg` in this folder) — most likely a scarf or tea towel in the British seaside textile-design tradition.

## Style Definition

**Pen-Coastal** is a hand-drawn pen-and-ink illustration style, screen-printed with deliberately **offset color registration**. Every motif is drawn first as loose, imperfect black ink linework — heavy cross-hatching for shading, slightly wobbly confident sketch lines, no ruler-straight edges — and only afterward given flat color washes that sit as a separate print layer *underneath* the linework. That second layer is never perfectly aligned to the first: color bleeds past an outline on one edge and falls short of it on another. This registration mismatch is not a flaw to fix — it is the entire visual signature of the style, and the single most important thing to get right in any generation.

## Lineage DNA

- **British seaside textile design** (mid-century tea towels, scarves, souvenir prints): coastal motifs — cottages, shells, rope, anchors — rendered as scattered vignettes rather than one scene
- **Screen-print / silkscreen registration aesthetic**: each color is a separate pass through the screen, hand-aligned, never pixel-perfect — Edward Bawden / Eric Ravilious-adjacent print tradition
- **Sketchbook illustration**: loose pen cross-hatch shading, visible construction lines, the confident imperfection of a fast observational drawing rather than a finished vector illustration

## The Core Technique — Offset Color Registration

This is the trait that makes or breaks the style. Without it, the result reads as generic "cute illustration" rather than Pen-Coastal.

- Ink linework is a **complete, self-sufficient line drawing** — it would look correct and finished even with zero color behind it.
- Color is a **flat, texture-free wash** applied as a rough rectangular or blob-shaped patch behind the linework, in a color unrelated to "correct" local color (roofs are sky-blue, walls are white, shadows are terracotta — not naturalistic shading).
- The color patch is **visibly misaligned** with the ink outline it's meant to fill: it overshoots the line on one or two edges (color visible outside the drawn outline) and undershoots on others (a sliver of white paper/canvas showing between the line and the color). Offsets are small — a few percent of the motif's size — not cartoonishly large.
- Some color patches are **drybrush-edged**, applied like a loose brushstroke with a visibly torn, uneven boundary rather than a hard geometric edge — especially for the teal background bands.
- Because of the offset, **linework and color read as two independent layers**, and the eye is meant to notice both: the "correct" black-ink drawing, and the "printed" color sitting slightly off from it.

**Prompt phrase**: "Hand-drawn black ink linework is a complete, self-sufficient line illustration; flat color washes are printed as a separate layer UNDER the linework and are deliberately misregistered — each color patch overshoots the ink outline on one or two edges and falls short of it on the others, small offset of a few percent, mimicking hand-aligned screen-print registration, NOT color filled precisely inside the lines like a coloring book"

## The Linework

- Pure black ink, uniform-ish weight with natural pen-pressure variation, never a clean vector stroke
- Confident, slightly wobbly, hand-drawn quality — visible sketch energy, not ruler-straight
- **Cross-hatching** is the primary shading technique: parallel and criss-crossed short strokes used for shell ridges, sand texture, roof thatch, shadow areas, fur/grass texture
- Scribbled loose linework for organic texture: dune grass tufts, thatch, wave crests — rendered as energetic overlapping scribbles, not individually drawn blades
- Thin single-line construction for structural elements: rope coils, window frames, rigging lines, sketchbook frame borders

**Prompt phrase**: "loose confident black ink pen linework with natural pressure variation, dense cross-hatching for shading and texture, scribbled energetic linework for grass and thatch, thin single-line strokes for rope and structural details, hand-drawn sketchbook quality, not vector-smooth"

## Palette

No pure black anywhere except the ink linework. Flat, muted, coastal.

| Role | Color | Notes |
|------|-------|-------|
| Background field | Dusty sky-blue / pale teal-blue | `#A9CBD1`–`#C7DEE2` range, sometimes drybrush-textured |
| Accent band | Deep teal / turquoise | `#2B7A85`–`#1F6570`, used for border bands and roof color washes |
| Structure | Navy / indigo | `#3B3A6E`–`#2A2860`, roof edges, window mullions, rope |
| Warm accent | Terracotta / coral-red | `#C85A42`–`#D9694E`, small pops — doors, shell markings, flags |
| Neutral fill | Sand tan | `#D8B98C`–`#C9A876`, cottage walls, shell bodies, roof tops |
| Ground | White / cream | Bare canvas showing through as the "paper" color |
| Border | Deep plum / aubergine | Occasionally used as an outer mat/border color, seen at the edges |

**Prompt phrase**: "muted coastal palette limited to dusty sky-blue, deep teal-turquoise, navy indigo, terracotta coral-red, sand tan, and cream white — no pure black except the ink linework, no bright saturated primary colors"

## Subject Motif Library

Draw from this vocabulary; mix 3–6 motifs per composition:

- **Gambrel-roofed beach cottages** — steep chevron/zigzag roofline, small square windows, a single red door or window awning accent, dune-grass scribble at the roofline
- **Whelk / conch seashells** — large, detailed, heavily cross-hatched ridges, terracotta banding
- **Coiled rope and knots** — thin navy double-line coil, loose figure-eight loops, frayed rope-end tufts
- **Anchors** — simple silhouette, cross-hatched shadow face
- **Portholes / life-rings** — circular ring motif with segment markings, sometimes a small red-and-white lifebuoy stripe pattern
- **Dune grass tufts** — scribbled radiating blade clusters, used as texture accents at roof-edges or ground-lines
- **Small bead/dot borders** — a row of tan or navy dots or ovals used as a trim line between compositional bands
- **Rowboats, buoys, fishing nets** — supplementary motifs in the same linework language when a piece needs more variety

## Composition

The source textile uses a **scattered vignette layout**: multiple small illustrated motifs placed across the canvas at different scales and rotations, sometimes enclosed in a thin hand-drawn rectangular "sketchbook frame," separated by dotted-bead trim lines and drybrush color bands. It reads like a page from an illustrated coastal notebook, not one unified scene.

This scatter layout is authentic to the reference, but it does **not** always translate well to a single hero piece of wall art (e.g., Samsung Frame TV), which benefits from one clear focal read from across a room. Offer the user a choice:

- **Vignette grid** (authentic to source) — 4–8 motifs from the library scattered at varied scale/rotation across the canvas, 1–2 motifs enclosed in thin hand-drawn sketchbook-frame rectangles, dotted-bead border trim separating regions, drybrush teal color bands as background structure. Best for a busy, textile/print-like piece, or when the user explicitly wants the "scarf/tea-towel" look.
- **Single hero motif** (recommended default for large-format wall art) — one motif from the library (a cottage, a large detailed shell, an anchor with rope) rendered big and centered or on a rule-of-thirds point, filling most of the frame, with the offset-registration color technique fully visible at large scale, and a plain or lightly banded background so the piece reads clearly from a distance.

**Prompt phrase (vignette grid)**: "scattered vignette composition with 5-6 coastal motifs at varied scale and slight rotation across the canvas, one or two motifs enclosed in a thin hand-drawn rectangular sketchbook frame border, a row of small dot-bead trim marks separating compositional bands, drybrush-edged teal color bands structuring the background"

**Prompt phrase (single hero motif)**: "a single large coastal motif filling most of the frame, placed at a rule-of-thirds point, rendered in full cross-hatched pen-and-ink detail with clearly visible offset color registration, set against a plain or subtly banded dusty sky-blue background so the motif reads clearly at a distance"

- **Copy-space / editorial banner** — for magazine spreads, web banners, or any use case where text will be overlaid on the piece. The subject cluster sits confidently in one third of the frame (left or right, picked deliberately, not centered), while the remaining half-to-two-thirds of the frame stays deliberately plain and uncluttered — flat sky, calm sea, or open sand with at most one small secondary element (a distant gull), no competing detail. The open zone isn't empty for its own sake — it's reserved negative space a designer can drop a headline or logo into without fighting the artwork. This differs from the single-hero-motif mode mainly in *where* the subject sits (off to a side, not centered/thirds-balanced) and in how aggressively empty the rest of the frame stays.

**Prompt phrase (copy-space / editorial banner)**: "strong asymmetric editorial composition with the [subject] positioned in the [left/right] third of the frame; the opposite half to two-thirds of the composition stays deliberately plain and uncluttered — flat sky meeting a calm sea at a clean horizon line, at most one small distant gull, no other visual clutter — leaving generous open negative space suitable for magazine or banner text overlay"

Loose confident black ink linework with natural pressure variation, dense cross-hatching for shading and texture, scribbled energetic linework for dune grass and thatch, hand-drawn sketchbook quality, not vector-smooth. ## Tips for great results

- **Always feed the bundled reference images** (`reference-Pen_Style_01.jpg` through `_03.jpg`) via `--reference-image` when generating — this style depends on a specific, unusual print technique (offset color registration) that is much easier for the model to copy from an example than to construct from a text description alone.
- **The offset-registration instruction is the one thing that must never be dropped** from the prompt. If it's missing, the model defaults to filling color neatly inside the lines like a coloring book, which kills the style.
- **If the result looks too clean/vector**, add "hand-drawn imperfection, slightly wobbly confident linework, visible pen pressure variation, NOT a clean vector illustration" to the prompt.
- **If the color sits perfectly inside the lines**, strengthen the offset instruction: "the color layer is VISIBLY misaligned with the ink linework — repeat, the flat color patches do not align precisely with the black outlines, small print-registration offset is essential to this style".
- **If cross-hatching is missing or too light**, add "dense, heavy cross-hatched shading with visible individual pen strokes" and name the specific area (shell ridges, roof shadow, sand texture).
- **For the vignette-grid composition**, cap it at 5-6 motifs — more than that and the model tends to lose linework quality per motif.
- **For the single-hero-motif composition**, this style still works well at Samsung Frame TV scale (3840x2160) because the cross-hatch detail and offset-color effect both read clearly at large size.
